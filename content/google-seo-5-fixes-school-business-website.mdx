**Google does not use the meta keywords tag, keyword stuffing is against its spam policies, and there are no secrets that will rank your site first.** That is not my opinion — it is Google's own SEO Starter Guide, written by the people who run the search engine. If your school or small business website is invisible on Google, the fix is probably free, and it is probably simpler than the SEO salesperson told you.

I build websites for schools and small businesses here in Bangkok, and I hear the same worry from almost every client: "We paid for a website, so why can't anyone find us on Google?" Usually the site is fine. It just never had the five basics done — the five things Google itself recommends in its starter guide.

## What Google's own guide actually says

The guide is Google Search Central's *SEO Starter Guide: The Basics*, and it opens with a sentence most SEO sales pitches would rather you never read: "There are no secrets here that'll automatically rank your site first in Google (sorry!)."

Here is what it does say works. First, **the vast majority of sites in Google's results are found and added automatically** as Google's crawlers explore the web — you usually do not need to do anything except publish your site. Most new pages Google finds every day, it finds through links from other pages. So being linked from somewhere real — your Facebook page, a school directory, a partner's site — matters more than any trick.

Second, the two things that decide whether a searcher clicks your result are the **title link** and the **snippet** — the headline and the short description under it. A good title is unique to the page, clear and concise, and accurately describes what the page offers, ideally with your business name and location. A good meta description is a short, one- or two-sentence summary of the page, unique to that page, covering its most relevant points.

Third, images count. Google says many people search visually, and images can be how someone finds your website for the first time. Every image should have **descriptive alt text** — a short piece of text explaining what the image shows — and sit near text that is relevant to it. Descriptive URLs help too: a page at `/admissions/apply.html` tells people and Google far more than `/2/6772756D707920636174`.

## Why most small websites get this wrong

The biggest mistake I see is spending money on the wrong things. A school pays for a "keyword package" that stuffs the same phrase into every paragraph. A shop owner buys a list of keywords to paste into a hidden meta tag. Google's guide lists both under "things we believe you shouldn't focus on": the keywords meta tag is ignored, and keyword stuffing is against Google's spam policies.

The second mistake is thinking SEO is a one-time job. Google's guide ends with a section on maintaining your site's presence over time, and its first recommended next step is setting up a **Search Console** account — the free tool that shows you how your site actually performs in Google Search and what is blocking it. It is free, it takes minutes to set up, and almost none of the small business sites I audit have it.

The third mistake is writing for Google instead of for the parent or customer. Google's guide says the content that influences your presence more than any other suggestion is content people find compelling and useful: easy to read, well organised, unique, up to date, and written for people first.

## In my client work

When a school or a small shop in Bangkok asks me to look at their website, I start the same way every time, and it takes about twenty minutes.

I search `site:` followed by their domain. If nothing comes back, I check the basics: is the site actually live, is there a robots.txt or a noindex tag blocking Google, does the homepage even have a title? I once audited a school site where every single page had the same title — just the school's name. Google had no way to tell the admissions page from the lunch menu.

Then I read the site the way a parent would. Does the homepage answer the three questions a visitor has in the first ten seconds: what is this place, who is it for, and what do I do next? If the answer is buried under a slider and three paragraphs of welcome message, I rewrite the top of the page before I touch anything technical.

## Steal this: the five fixes, in order

Do these five things this week, in this order, and you will have done more real SEO than most paid packages deliver.

**1. Check you are indexed.** Search Google for `site:yourdomain.com`. If your pages appear, skip to step 2. If not, check with whoever built your site that nothing is blocking Google, then submit a sitemap through Search Console.

**2. Fix your page titles.** Every page gets its own title: unique, clear, and honest about what is on the page, with your business or school name and your location. In WordPress, your SEO plugin or your theme settings let you edit this per page — no code needed.

**3. Write one meta description per important page.** One or two sentences, unique to that page, saying what the visitor will find. That is the text Google may show under your title in the results, so write it for the parent, not the algorithm.

**4. Describe your images and clean up your URLs.** Add alt text to every image — "Year 3 students building a robot with micro:bit" — and rename pages so the URL says what the page is. In WordPress, alt text is a field on the image when you upload it.

**5. Set up Google Search Console.** It is free. Verify your site, then check the reports it gives you: which pages Google has indexed, which search terms bring people to you, and which pages have errors. Check it once a month.

## The honest caveat

Google is explicit that following this guide does not guarantee your site will be added to its index, and it does not guarantee any ranking. Some changes take months to show up. SEO is slow, unglamorous maintenance — and that is exactly why it is worth doing properly instead of buying shortcuts. If someone guarantees you a ranking, they are guaranteeing something Google says it does not sell.
