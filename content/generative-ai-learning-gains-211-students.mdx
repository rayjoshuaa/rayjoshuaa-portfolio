**AI access raised test scores by 6.7 points — and about 76% of that gain was still there a week later, after the AI was taken away.** But the gain only lasted for students who asked AI to explain things. The students who got AI to do the work for them lost it all.

Last week I wrote about the AI learning penalty — the study of 26,811 students where homework got better and exam scores fell 20%. This week, a new randomised experiment tells the other half of the story. AI is not automatically good or bad for learning. The difference is what the student asks it to do.

## What the experiment actually found

The research comes from Zara Contractor and Germán Reyes at Middlebury College, published in July 2026 as *Experimental Evidence on the Learning Impact of Generative AI*. The team ran a randomised experiment with **211 undergraduates**. In the first session, students studied an unfamiliar technical topic and wrote an analytical essay — half with access to any off-the-shelf generative AI tool, half without. About a week later, everyone took another test and wrote another essay on the same topic, this time with no AI and no other resources.

The headline numbers:

- Students with AI access scored **6.7 percentage points higher** on the immediate knowledge test, on a baseline of 56.3%. That is a gain of **0.27 standard deviations** — a solid, meaningful effect in education research.
- The gain persisted: one week later, with no AI allowed, the same students still scored **5.1 points higher** on the retention test — about **76% of the immediate effect survived**.
- The essays told a slower story. While students still had AI, their essay quality barely improved. A week later, writing unaided, their essays improved significantly in style, clarity and relevance to the prompt. The learning showed up after the tool was gone.

## Explainers gained. Automators did not.

The most useful finding is the split inside the AI group — the researchers read the students' ChatGPT logs and sorted them into two kinds of user.

**Augmentation users** — about **49% of AI users** — worked with the AI. They asked it to explain concepts, which was the most common use overall at **57% of treated students**. Drafting responses came second at **31%**, and summarising readings third at **17%**.

**Automation users** — about **32% of AI users** — used AI to do the work for them, mostly by getting it to draft their essays.

The two groups ended up in very different places. The augmentation users' test-score gains persisted a week after the AI was removed, and their unaided essays a week later trended higher in quality. The automation users' short-run essay gains faded entirely once the tool was gone.

The researchers also traced why the explainers learned more. AI access did not change total learning time — it changed what the time was spent on. Treated students spent **12% less time writing** and **10% more time reading and searching for information**, and reported **13% higher enjoyment** of the learning. Less drafting, more reading, more enjoyment: that is what a tutor does, not what a ghostwriter does.

This lines up with the study I covered last week. In that Chinese secondary-school study, the students who kept spending roughly as long on homework as non-users kept their exam scores. The ones who finished in under 50 minutes with perfect homework lost 20% on exams. Two different studies, two different countries, one pattern: **AI helps when it does the explaining, and harms when it does the thinking.**

## In my classroom

I teach ICT, Computer Science and Robotics in Bangkok, and I see this exact split every week.

In my Year 7 and 8 Python lessons, I now teach the difference out loud. When a student hits an error, there are two prompts they can type. "Fix this code for me" is the automation prompt — the chatbot rewrites the loop, the program runs, and the student has learned nothing. "Explain why this loop never ends, and give me a hint, not the answer" is the augmentation prompt. The student still has to fix the code themselves, but now they understand what a loop condition actually does. The first student is stuck again next week when the same bug appears in a different program. The second student is not.

Robotics makes it even clearer. A student who lets AI write the whole Arduino sketch for a line-following robot has nothing to fall back on when the robot veers off the track during a class demo. The student who asked AI to explain how an infrared sensor reads the line, then wrote the logic themselves, knows exactly which line to check.

So in my classroom the rule is simple: AI explains, students decide. The chatbot is a tutor you can ask anything, not a classmate you copy from.

## Steal this: four routines that turn AI into a tutor

**1. Teach the two prompts side by side.** Show students "write my essay" next to "explain this concept to me like I am 12, then quiz me on it." Let them feel the difference. Most students have never been shown that the prompt is the choice.

**2. Ask for explanations, then close the chat.** The routine is: ask AI to explain, read the explanation, close the laptop, and explain it back in your own words. If you cannot, you did not learn it — you only read it. That is the pattern that produced the lasting gains.

**3. Ban copy-paste, keep copy-understand.** Any AI output a student uses must be rewritten in their own words. Copy-paste is where learning goes to die. In the experiment, the automation users' essays were flagged as AI-generated far more often — and their gains vanished.

**4. Test a week later, without the tool.** Short closed-book quizzes on last week's AI-assisted work show what actually stuck. The experiment's most important measurement was the test a week later, with no AI allowed. That is the test that matters in your classroom too.

## The honest caveat

This is one experiment with 211 undergraduates, on one unfamiliar topic, over about one week. The authors are careful about that. There is also a warning inside the results: the gains were larger for students in the upper ability quartiles, measured by GPA and SAT scores, than for students at the bottom. If only the strongest students benefit, AI access could widen learning gaps rather than close them — which means the students who need the most support are exactly the ones we have to teach to use it well. AI access also raised rule violations on the first test, though the authors say those accounted for only a modest share of the gains.

Last week the penalty was real. This week the gain is real too. Same technology, opposite outcomes — and the student holding the keyboard decides which one they get.

Which of your students is using AI as a tutor, and which one is using it as a ghostwriter — and how would you know?
