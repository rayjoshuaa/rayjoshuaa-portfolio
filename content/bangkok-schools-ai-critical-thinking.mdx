Every school is being sold AI tools right now. Very few are stopping to agree on the more important question first: *what should change about how our students think?*

That is why a piece of news from my own city caught my attention. On 8 October 2026, the Bangkok Primary Educational Service Area Office, the Institute for Academic Quality Development (IAQD) and school administrators signed a memorandum of understanding covering **31 Bangkok schools**, committing them to rework teaching for the AI era. The centre of the agreement is not a platform or a device. It is a thinking process: **GPAS 5 Steps**.

## What GPAS 5 Steps actually is

GPAS is a Thai active-learning model that structures a lesson as a thinking journey rather than a delivery of content:

1. **Gathering** — students gather and select information that matters, instead of receiving it pre-chewed.
2. **Processing** — they organise that information, spot patterns and connections, and create meaning from it.
3. **Applying 1: constructing knowledge** — they plan, act and solve problems, building their own understanding by using it.
4. **Applying 2: communication** — they express and defend their ideas, because knowledge you cannot explain is not yet yours.
5. **Self-regulation** — they monitor their own progress, reflect, and improve without waiting to be told.

Read those five steps again with AI in mind and you can see why Bangkok's schools chose it. A chatbot can flood a student with information in seconds. Gathering *critically*, processing honestly, applying genuinely and self-regulating are exactly the parts a machine cannot do for them — and exactly the parts school must now protect and train.

## The shift I love most: teachers become coaches

The agreement is explicit that AI is there to *support* education, not to run it — from curriculum design through to teacher competency and training. And it asks for a role change that I think is the heart of AI-era teaching: educators become **coaches and mentors** who inspire students to think, research and innovate, rather than answer machines competing with a chatbot that always answers faster.

IAQD's Paisarn Rojnasranrom made the student side of the same point: the goal is to move learners from passive recipients of information to **active creators of knowledge**. The collaboration also deliberately reaches beyond the staffroom — administrators, teachers, parents and the wider community are all part of it, schools will build professional learning communities to share what works, and ethical AI use is named as a shared commitment, not an afterthought.

Two details are worth noticing. First, the reform starts with **people**: developing administrators and teachers is treated as essential to changing outcomes, before any classroom technology. Second, one of its stated aims is students' *technological resilience* — young people who can keep thinking clearly as the tools keep changing.

## What I am taking from this into my own teaching

I teach ICT and Robotics here in Bangkok, so this is not an abstract policy story for me. Three things from this agreement are already shaping how I think about my classroom:

**1. Start with the thinking process, not the tool.** If a lesson begins with "open the AI app," the thinking has already been outsourced. If it begins with a GPAS-style question — what do we need to find out, and how will we judge what we find? — the AI becomes one source among several, used at the Gathering and Processing steps, under the student's control.

**2. Design my role as coach on purpose.** In a robotics or coding lesson, my most useful moves are rarely giving answers. They are the coaching prompts: *What have you tried? What did the robot actually do, not what you expected? Show me your evidence.* The Bangkok agreement simply names, at system level, what good teachers already feel lesson by lesson.

**3. Do ethics and experiments together, not alone.** Professional learning communities and shared ethical-AI commitments mean a teacher does not have to work out alone what is fair, what is safe, and what actually helps learning. Schools that share prompts, failures and policies will move faster — and more safely — than schools where every teacher improvises in private.

## The real takeaway

Real AI reform does not start with buying another tool. It starts with school leaders and teachers agreeing on how students should think, what teachers are *for*, and where AI is allowed to help — and then signing their names to it, together, the way these 31 Bangkok schools just did.

If your school made one AI-era change this term, what should it be?
